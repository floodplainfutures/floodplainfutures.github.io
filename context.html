floodplain futures

An interactive design project looking at the Yolo Basin as a working landscape where flooding, agriculture, and migration overlap rather than compete.

01

premise

Hi! I’m Zahra Baxi, an MFA Design student at UC Davis and a Davis Native. This project started from living just less than 15 minutes away from an amazing landscape that shifts between flood control, farmland, and wildlife habitat. I lived here for 20+ years without really understanding what was going on in that space and never truly visited until I started this thesis.

The Yolo Basin is one of those places that doesn’t behave like a single system. In winter it becomes a managed floodplain. In spring and summer it returns to agriculture. Throughout the year it also functions as a critical stop along the Pacific Flyway for migrating birds.

I started this thesis by trying to understand how a place can be designed to hold all of these roles at once, and what gets lost when we only see it as infrastructure or only as nature.

“The same ground can hold floodwater, food production, and migration, depending on how we choose to see it.”

field notes, Yolo Basin research

This project sits in between ecology, design, and systems thinking. It looks at how information design can help people understand a landscape that is constantly changing shape and purpose.

02

the place

41 mi bypass length
59,000 acres
200+ bird species annually

The Yolo Basin Wildlife Area sits just outside Davis and Sacramento, shaped by engineered flood control systems that redirect seasonal water from the Sacramento River.

During wet months, the basin is intentionally flooded to relieve pressure on nearby cities. In dry months, the same land supports rice fields and other agricultural production. These shifts are not accidental, they are designed into the landscape.

Because of this cycle, the basin also becomes one of the most important wetland habitats in the region, supporting migratory birds traveling along the Pacific Flyway.

infrastructure

The basin is part of a controlled flood system that protects surrounding cities from seasonal overflow.

ecology

Flooded fields temporarily become wetlands that support fish, insects, and aquatic plants.

agriculture

Rice farming uses the same flood infrastructure during dry months, linking food production with water management.

migration

Hundreds of thousands of birds rely on the basin as seasonal habitat and resting ground.

03

the question

How do you design systems that help people understand a landscape that is not fixed, but constantly shifting between infrastructure, ecology, and agriculture?

Most representations of floodplains separate these functions into categories. Flood control is engineering. Wetlands are ecology. Farms are agriculture. But in places like the Yolo Basin, these boundaries blur in practice.

?

What happens when we design tools that show overlap instead of separation, and how does that change the way we value a landscape?

This thesis explores whether interactive design can make these overlapping systems easier to see, and whether that visibility can shift how people relate to land that is usually treated as background infrastructure.

04

method

This project combines field research, mapping, and interaction design to translate ecological systems into visual and digital form.

I started by visiting the Yolo Basin during different seasons, documenting how the landscape changes visually and functionally over time. That fieldwork became the foundation for mapping and data collection.

  1. 01
    field observation

    Site visits across seasons to document flooding patterns, wildlife presence, and agricultural cycles.

  2. 02
    mapping systems

    Translating environmental data into layered spatial maps that show overlapping land uses.

  3. 03
    interaction design

    Building an interactive experience that allows users to explore the basin as a changing system.

  4. 04
    visual translation

    Designing visuals that connect scientific data with accessible storytelling.

The goal is not to simplify the basin, but to make its complexity easier to approach without flattening what is actually happening there.

05

thesis

This thesis looks at how design can make ecological systems more legible without removing their complexity.

thesis statement

This project explores how interactive and information design can bridge ecological research and public understanding by translating the Yolo Basin into an accessible, layered system that reflects its overlapping roles as flood control infrastructure, agricultural land, and wildlife habitat.

Using a systems-based design approach, the work focuses on making environmental data experiential rather than abstract, so people can understand how the basin operates over time rather than as a static map.

The intention is not to resolve the contradictions in the landscape, but to show that they are the structure of it.
