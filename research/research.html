the basin is alive

The Yolo Bypass is not static infrastructure—it's a living system that inhales water in winter and exhales it by summer, breathing life into thousands of migratory birds. This is one year in the life of 59,000 acres.

winter: the flood

December through February brings heavy rains. The Sacramento River spills through the Fremont Weir, and the bypass becomes a shallow inland sea. Tens of thousands of geese and ducks arrive, turning the flooded grassland into one of California's most important waterfowl habitats.

spring: the drawdown

As winter storms end, water slowly recedes. This is when the bypass becomes most productive: exposed mudflats teem with invertebrates, creating a feast for thousands of shorebirds. Sandpipers, plovers, and phalaropes stop here on their way to Arctic breeding grounds.

summer: the drying

By July, most of the bypass is dry grassland again. Water retreats to managed wetlands in the Wildlife Area. Migratory birds have left. A few resident herons and egrets remain, hunting in shrinking pools. The landscape waits.

fall: the return

October brings the first rains. Early migrants—pintails, wigeons, teal—return. The bypass prepares for another cycle. The rhythm continues, year after year, supporting millions of birds along the Pacific Flyway.

about this visualization

the data

Water levels: USGS Station 11453000 provides real-time discharge measurements from the Yolo Bypass near Woodland. We use 22 years (2004-2026) of daily readings, aggregated by month.

Bird observations: eBird is a global database of citizen science bird observations. We analyze over 50,000 checklists from Yolo Bypass hotspots to calculate monthly observation frequency for each species.

the visualization

Each bird is represented as an individual mark on the canvas, similar to how each observation represents a real sighting. Water flows and accumulates organically using particle physics. The landscape transformation mirrors the actual seasonal cycle.

Colors represent species groups: waterfowl (blue/purple), shorebirds (pink/red), herons (olive/yellow). Density patterns show where birds concentrate as conditions change.

the story

The Yolo Bypass wasn't designed to be a bird sanctuary—it's flood control infrastructure. But by mimicking the seasonal wetlands that once covered California's Central Valley, it accidentally became one of the most important bird habitats on the Pacific Flyway.

This visualization shows how infrastructure, when designed with natural processes in mind, can serve multiple purposes: protecting cities from floods while also protecting wildlife.
