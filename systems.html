Three things happen in the Yolo Basin every year: it floods, birds show up, and rice gets grown. They depend on each other more than you might expect. This page traces that connection through real data from the basin.

Look at 2022. A bad drought year hit Yolo County hard: rice acreage dropped from about 17,800 acres to just 9,507, the lowest in the six-year period, because farmers couldn't get enough water to plant. The Fremont Weir did not overtop at all. Bird activity at the wildlife area dropped too. Then 2023 came with record winter rains and everything reversed. Rice hit 29,974 acres, the Fremont Weir overtopped for 73 days, and checklists climbed right alongside it. All three bars move in the same direction at the same time.

rice in yolo county

Six years of rice data from the Yolo County Agricultural Commissioner's Crop Report. After each harvest, the same fields get flooded on purpose. That water is what the birds come for all winter. Select a year to see what happened.

After harvest, rice fields in the bypass are flooded from October through March. Farmers receive payments through state and federal wetland programs to keep the fields wet. The leftover grain feeds waterfowl. The flooded stalks decompose into the invertebrates that shorebirds eat. In a wet year, that managed flooding layers on top of the natural Fremont Weir floods, and the basin becomes one of the most productive bird stopovers on the West Coast.

Source: Yolo County Department of Agriculture, Weights & Measures — Annual Crop & Livestock Reports 2018–2023. Yolo County data only. yolocounty.gov/ag

05

how it connects

01

The river goes over the weir

When the Sacramento River runs high enough, it spills over the Fremont Weir, a 1.8-mile structure on the north end of the bypass. That is when the basin floods. Habitat managers look for at least 14 days of strong flow to support wildlife. In 2023, the basin stayed flooded for 73 days.

02

Birds find the water

Flooded fields mean food. Shallow water exposes the insects and seeds that shorebirds and waterfowl depend on. Over 200 species show up at the Yolo Bypass Wildlife Area each year. The winter visitors (cranes, pintails, dunlin) are here because the fields are wet. That is the whole reason.

03

The rice fields stay wet after harvest

Farmers flood their harvested fields each fall partly as straw management and partly because there are programs that pay them to do it for wildlife. It is cheaper than burning the stubble. The leftover rice feeds ducks and geese, and the decomposing stalks feed the bugs that shorebirds need. In a wet year, this managed flooding and the natural bypass flooding stack on top of each other.

food

In 2022, farmers planted 59% less rice because the water simply was not there. The Fremont Weir did not overtop at all. Fewer flooded fields meant less habitat, and bird numbers at the wildlife area reflected that. When the rains came back in 2023, the rice came back. And so did the birds.
